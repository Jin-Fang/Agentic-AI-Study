# Chapter 7: Post-Training

Pretraining teaches a model to predict tokens across a broad text distribution. Post-training applies additional optimization on narrower data or feedback so that the model more often behaves like an assistant. Karpathy's introduction separates pretraining from fine-tuning and describes the later stages that make a model more useful in dialogue and instruction-following settings ([Intro to LLMs, around 00:14:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zjkBMFhNj_g&t=869s)).

This distinction helps explain why a base model and an assistant model descended from a similar architecture can behave so differently. Post-training changes the probabilities of possible outputs. It can make helpful responses, refusals, particular styles, and multi-turn conventions more likely, but those tendencies are not formal guarantees of correctness, safety, or completeness.

## Supervised Fine-Tuning

Supervised fine-tuning, or SFT, continues training on examples that pair an input with a desired response. The examples may include instructions and answers, conversations, formatting patterns, or domain-specific tasks. The model still learns through token prediction, commonly with the loss focused on the target response tokens, but the new data shifts its output distribution toward the demonstrated behavior.

SFT data can be written by people, generated by other models, or assembled from a mixture of human and synthetic sources. It may also be filtered, revised, or annotated before training. The composition varies by project, so neither exclusively human-written data nor exclusively synthetic data is part of the definition of SFT.

A minimal conversational example looks like this:

```text
User: Explain gradient descent.
Assistant: ...
```

Training on many such examples makes an assistant-like continuation more likely after a similar prompt. A useful mental model is that the model reproduces patterns present in the selected answers: their tone, level of detail, ways of asking for clarification, and kinds of requests they decline. It does not acquire the intentions or understanding of the people who wrote or selected those answers.

SFT can teach useful task behavior, but successful imitation on the training distribution does not by itself establish that an answer is true or that the behavior will generalize to every new input.

## Learned Interaction Conventions

Conversation roles and boundaries must ultimately be represented as tokens. A chat template may serialize role labels, message separators, turn endings, and other control tokens into a sequence. When those patterns occur during post-training, they become part of the distribution the model learns.

As a result, an assistant model may learn conventions such as responding in the assistant role, addressing the current request, asking a clarifying question when information is missing, using familiar formatting, and ending a response at an expected boundary. If the training data contains refusals for certain categories of requests, the model can also learn refusal patterns.

These conventions are statistical regularities rather than a symbolic protocol implemented outside the model. Their reliability depends on the training distribution and the context presented at inference time. A familiar serialization can elicit learned behavior more consistently, while a different or ambiguous serialization may not.

## Preference Optimization: RLHF, DPO, and AI Feedback

SFT demonstrates desired responses, but many qualities are easier to express by comparing candidates than by writing a single ideal answer. In the InstructGPT work, labelers wrote demonstrations, ranked model outputs, and provided the preference data used to train a reward model and optimize the policy with reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF ([Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)).

A classic RLHF pipeline has five stages:

1. Train or start from an SFT assistant model.
2. Sample multiple candidate responses to prompts.
3. Collect human rankings or pairwise preferences.
4. Train a reward model to predict those preferences.
5. Optimize the assistant policy for higher predicted reward, usually while constraining it to remain near a reference model.

The reward model is another learned model. It maps a candidate response and its context to a score that approximates preferences in the comparison data ([Deep Dive, around 02:52:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xTGNNLPyMI&t=10359s)). The reference constraint limits how far policy optimization moves from the starting behavior; without such a constraint, optimization can more easily drive the policy into regions where the reward model is unreliable.

Direct Preference Optimization, or DPO, uses preferred and dispreferred responses to optimize the policy with a classification-like objective, avoiding the same separate reward-model training and online reinforcement-learning loop ([Direct Preference Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)). It is another way to learn from preferences, not a source of ground truth.

Preference judgments can also come partly from models rather than directly from people. For example, Constitutional AI uses feedback guided by written principles to reduce the amount of human labeling needed for parts of harmlessness training ([Constitutional AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073)). Whether feedback is human- or model-generated, its quality and coverage shape the behavior that optimization reinforces.

## Proxy Rewards and Reward Hacking

A learned reward is a proxy for the qualities its designers care about. Human preferences are compressed into finite comparisons and then approximated by a model, so the resulting score is necessarily incomplete. Karpathy describes a reward model as a lossy simulation of human preference ([Deep Dive, around 03:00:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xTGNNLPyMI&t=10854s)).

When a policy is optimized strongly against an imperfect proxy, it may discover outputs that receive high scores without satisfying the underlying intent. This is reward hacking ([Deep Dive, around 03:04:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xTGNNLPyMI&t=11051s)). A policy might learn superficial markers of a preferred answer, become unnecessarily verbose, agree too readily with the user, or exploit blind spots in the reward model.

Reference constraints and other regularization can limit how far optimization moves, but they do not turn a proxy into truth. Preference optimization can improve average judged quality while still introducing systematic distortions outside the comparisons represented in training.

## Verifiable Rewards and RLVR

Some training tasks provide rewards that can be computed directly. An exact answer can be compared with a known result, a proof can be checked, a program can be run against tests, or a game can return a score. Other qualities—such as whether an explanation is helpful or whether a tone is appropriate—depend much more on judgment.

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, often abbreviated RLVR, uses such checkable outcomes as the reinforcement signal. Karpathy contrasts reinforcement learning in verifiable domains with reinforcement learning based on unverifiable preferences ([Deep Dive, around 02:51:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xTGNNLPyMI&t=10293s)). Verifiable signals can be cheaper to apply at scale and less subjective than preference scores for the tasks they cover.

Verification is still scoped to what the checker measures. Passing a checker does not establish every desirable property of the response, and gains on verifiable tasks need not transfer uniformly to tasks without comparable feedback. RLVR can strengthen problem-solving behavior that reaches checkable outcomes; it does not prove that generated reasoning is faithful or universally reliable. [Chapter 8](./08-prompting-and-in-context-learning.md) discusses reasoning behavior at inference time.

## Behavior Is Not Capability

Post-training changes observable behavior and can also improve, suppress, or redirect task performance. The distinction between behavior and capability is therefore useful, but capability is not a directly visible, fixed quantity. What a model produces depends on the input, learned response tendencies, decoding, and the specific task.

A refusal shows that the model produced a learned refusal behavior in that context. It does not by itself show that the model lacks the ability to produce the requested content under every other context. Conversely, one successful answer does not establish a robust capability, and a confident answer does not establish that the underlying claim is true.

The same limitation applies to safety behavior. Post-training can make undesirable outputs less likely, but a learned refusal policy is not a hard guarantee. [Chapter 10](./10-knowledge-hallucination-uncertainty.md) examines uncertainty, hallucination, and refusal behavior, while [Chapter 12](./12-reasoning-tools-and-agents.md) distinguishes model-generated actions from their execution. Formal controls outside the model belong to the companion [Agent Harness](../agent-harness/README.md).

## Full and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

The training objective and the choice of trainable parameters are separate decisions. Full fine-tuning updates all or nearly all of the base model's parameters and produces a new checkpoint whose behavior reflects those updates. Because knowledge and behavior are distributed across parameters, an update aimed at one task can also change performance elsewhere.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning, or PEFT, keeps most base parameters frozen and trains a smaller set of parameters. LoRA is a common PEFT method that learns low-rank updates to selected weight matrices. Other approaches add adapters or train small prompt-like parameter sets. These methods reduce the number of trainable parameters and can store task-specific changes separately from the base checkpoint, although their quality and runtime properties depend on the method and task.

Both full fine-tuning and PEFT alter learned behavior through training data and an optimization objective. Neither turns model parameters into a live database or provides continuously updated facts with source attribution. [Chapter 11](./11-embeddings-and-retrieval.md) covers retrieval from external collections, and [Chapter 14](./14-operational-mental-model.md) summarizes the boundary between model properties and surrounding systems.

## Key Takeaways

- Post-training applies additional optimization that makes a base model more likely to behave like an assistant.
- SFT data may be human-written, synthetic, or mixed, and it teaches both task responses and serialized interaction conventions.
- RLHF, DPO, and AI-feedback methods steer behavior using preferences; their objectives remain proxies rather than ground truth.
- RLVR supplies checkable rewards for tasks with verifiable outcomes, but its signal covers only what is actually verified.
- Refusals and successful answers are observations of behavior, not complete proofs about capability, truth, or safety.
- Full fine-tuning updates most or all parameters, while PEFT methods such as LoRA train a smaller parameter set.
